# Reading the *Taḥkemoni*: background for the general reader

Judah al-Ḥarizi wrote the *Taḥkemoni* for readers who knew the Hebrew Bible by heart, read Arabic literature for
pleasure, and enjoyed a good trick. Most readers today have only some of these. This note gives the background they are
most likely to need: who al-Ḥarizi was, what a maqama is, how the book is built, the world it describes, and how to
read this edition.

*Written 25-Nov-2026 with Claude (Anthropic). The facts about al-Ḥarizi's life follow the works listed at the end;
the description of the gates follows this site's translation. Corrections are welcome on
[GitHub](https://github.com/openseforim/tahkemoni/issues).*

## 1. Judah al-Ḥarizi

Judah ben Solomon al-Ḥarizi was born in Christian Spain about 1165, probably in Toledo, into a family that came from
the south (the name may point to Jerez). He grew up in two languages and two literatures: the Hebrew of the Bible and
the synagogue, and the Arabic of the Islamic world, which the Jews of Spain had used for science, philosophy and
everyday life for three centuries.

In his first career he was a translator from Arabic into Hebrew, and a very good one. He translated Maimonides'
commentary on the Mishnah (in part) and his *Guide of the Perplexed* (as *Moreh Nevukhim*, a version that competed with
that of Samuel ibn Tibbon), works of ethics and philosophy, and, most important for this book, the *Maqāmāt* of the
Arabic writer al-Ḥarīrī of Basra (died 1122). His Hebrew version of al-Ḥarīrī is called *Maḥberot Itiel*. Working on it
convinced him that Hebrew could do everything Arabic did, and he set out to prove it with a book of his own.

About 1215 he left Spain for good. He went by sea to Egypt (Alexandria and Cairo), then to the Land of Israel
(Jerusalem, about 1217; Acre), Damascus, Mosul, Baghdad and other cities of Iraq, and finally to Aleppo in Syria. He
lived from his pen: he wrote poems of praise for patrons in each Jewish community, and poems of blame for those who
did not pay. An Arab contemporary, Ibn al-Shaʿʿār of Mosul, included him in a dictionary of the poets of his time; this
notice, found and published by Joseph Sadan, gives the only description of him by someone who met him, and the date of
his death, in Aleppo in 1225. His travels are also told in his own Hebrew and Arabic writings, which J. Yahalom and
J. Blau published together in 2002.

## 2. What a maqama is

The *maqāma* (plural *maqāmāt*; in Hebrew *maḥberet*, and al-Ḥarizi's own word *sha'ar*, "gate") is an Arabic form
invented by al-Hamadhānī (died 1008) and made classic by al-Ḥarīrī. It has three fixed features:

- **Rhymed prose.** The text runs in short units, each ending in a rhyme with the unit before it (in Arabic *saj'*;
  this edition prints one rhyme unit per line). Poems in strict metre are set into the prose.
- **A narrator and a hero.** In each episode the same narrator meets the same hero, a wandering master of eloquence
  who lives by his wits, often in disguise, and who is recognized only at the end. In al-Ḥarīrī they are al-Ḥārith ibn
  Hammām and Abū Zayd of Sarūj.
- **Display.** The point is the language: word play, rare words, set speeches, debates, riddles, letters that can be
  read two ways, and poems with tricks of letters.

Al-Ḥarizi kept all three and made them Hebrew. His narrator is **Heman the Ezrahite** (a singer and sage of King
Solomon's time, 1 Kgs 5:11, Ps 88:1); his hero is **Ḥever the Kenite** (the husband of Jael, Judg 4:11, whose name also
means "companion" and suggests *meḥabber*, "author"). The book's title comes from 2 Sam 23:8, where one of David's
heroes is called "the Taḥkemonite"; it also suggests *ḥokhmah*, wisdom.

## 3. Biblical language: *shibbutz*

Al-Ḥarizi builds his Hebrew almost entirely from pieces of the Bible, set into new places and often bent to new
senses (*shibbutz*, "inlay"). A drunkard may speak in the words of the prophets; a verse about the Temple may describe a
banquet. Much of the humour lies in the distance between a verse's first place and its new one. This translation gives
the source of a phrase in gray brackets, for example [Ps 52:4], when the translator is sure of it, with the chapter and
verse numbers of the Hebrew Bible (these sometimes differ by a verse or two from English Bibles, above all in Psalms).
Where a verse is bent, the English gives the new sense and the bracket shows the old one.

Puns are everywhere, and most cannot be carried into English. A note marked ⚠ explains the ones that matter for the
sense.

## 4. How the book is built

The *Taḥkemoni* has an introduction and fifty gates. In the introduction al-Ḥarizi praises God and the soul, laments
that the Jews have forgotten their own language for Arabic, and tells how a vision of Hebrew as a lovely and neglected
bride sent him to write. Gate 1 tells how the book came to be and praises a patron. The other gates are separate
episodes; some kinds recur:

- **Tricks of Ḥever.** He appears as a preacher, a doctor, a cantor, an astrologer or a poor old man, wins money or a
  meal, and is unmasked (for example gates 2, 6, 10, 21, 24, 30).
- **Contests and debates.** Poets compete; or two things argue which is better: the pen and the sword, night and day,
  man and woman, sea and land, miserliness and generosity, soul and body (gates 4, 9, 13, 16, 19, 39–43).
- **Displays of skill.** A letter that praises when read forward and curses when read backward (gate 8); a letter in
  which every word has the letter resh, and one in which none has it (gate 11); a poem in Hebrew, Arabic and Aramaic;
  alphabet poems (gate 33).
- **Prayers and serious pieces.** Two long prayers (gates 14–15), a sermon on the vanity of the world (gate 2),
  Jerusalem (gate 28), the tomb of Ezra (gate 35).
- **Literary history and travel.** Gates 3 and 18 survey the Hebrew poets of Spain, from Samuel ha-Nagid and Solomon
  ibn Gabirol to Moses ibn Ezra and Judah Halevi; gate 46 describes the Jewish communities that al-Ḥarizi knew, from
  Toledo, Barcelona and Provence to Alexandria, Cairo, the Land of Israel, Damascus, Aleppo, Mosul and Baghdad, city by
  city, with praise and satire.
- **Collections of poems.** The last gate gathers many separate poems.

The gates do not form a single story, and they can be read in any order.

## 5. The world of the book

The *Taḥkemoni* was finished in the East about 1220, in the years after the Third Crusade and during the Fifth. Much
of what Ḥever and Heman see belongs to the Ayyubid world of Saladin's heirs: Jewish communities in Cairo, Damascus,
Aleppo, Mosul and Baghdad, each with its leaders, its scholars, its rich men and its quarrels; Arabic as the language of
government and learning; travel by caravan and by ship; patrons who paid poets for praise. The Land of Israel appears
as a land of ruins and pilgrims: al-Ḥarizi reached Jerusalem soon after the Ayyubids allowed Jews to resettle there,
and wrote of it with both joy and grief (gate 28). Ezra's tomb in Iraq (gate 35) was a place of pilgrimage for Jews
and Muslims alike.

Some figures recur: the **nasi** (a title of communal leaders who claimed descent from David), the **rosh yeshivah**,
the **ḥazzan** or prayer leader, the rich patron (*nadiv*, "generous one"), the scholar, the physician. Al-Ḥarizi
praises and mocks all of them, and his own complaint, that the generous have died and only the miserly remain, runs
through the book.

## 6. Versions and the history of the text

Al-Ḥarizi revised the *Taḥkemoni* more than once during his last years, and changed the dedications to suit new
patrons; manuscripts differ in the order of the gates and in some contents (see the study by M. Rand). The book was
first printed in Constantinople in 1578. Modern editions:

- Paul de Lagarde (Göttingen, 1883), from manuscripts, unvocalized.
- A. Kaminka (Warsaw, 1899), with an introduction and commentary.
- Y. Toporowsky (Tel Aviv: Maḥbarot le-Sifrut with Mosad ha-Rav Kook, 1952), vocalized; this is the text that Project
  Ben-Yehuda reproduces.
- J. Yahalom and N. Katsumata (Jerusalem: Ben-Zvi Institute, 2010), the first critical edition, with commentary.

## 7. How to read this edition

- **Hebrew.** The Hebrew is the vocalized text of Project Ben-Yehuda (from Toporowsky's edition), one rhyme unit per
  line in the prose and one verse line per line in the poems, with the two halves of a verse line set apart. Gate 35 is
  not on Ben-Yehuda and is taken from Kaminka's edition. The end of every gate was compared with Kaminka's edition;
  where the Ben-Yehuda text stops early (gates 5, 21 and 23), the missing passage is added from Kaminka, unvocalized,
  and marked on the page.
- **English.** One English line for each Hebrew line, close to the Hebrew, with the sense first and occasional rhyme.
  It is by Claude (Anthropic), directed and reviewed by Ezra Brand.
- **Brackets and notes.** [Gray brackets] give biblical and rabbinic sources. ⚠ marks a rendering that is uncertain or
  a pun that the English cannot keep; the note is at the foot of the page.
- **Speakers.** "Thus says Heman the Ezrahite" opens most gates; "Said the teller" marks the narrator's return.
