Thus says Heman the Ezrahite: I journeyed while I was still a tender youth
from the land of Hadrach [Zech 9:1]
to the Holy City, where the lamp of the kingship is set in order,
and the light of the Presence has trodden.
There I joined company with the chosen of God,
and there I met with the children of Israel.
And it came to pass after some days,
as I sat in an assembly of sages,
honored and high,
who raised poetry up to the heavens
and poured rains from the streams of its themes,
that they were speaking of the matter of poetry and its secrets:
to know how its foundations were first built,
and who dug its springs for the Jews,
and made its lights shine for them.
Some of them answered and said: "Behold, we all know,
and our fathers have told us [Ps 44:2],
that poetry is the inheritance of the Arabs,
from the day they were upon their land.
But they did not know when the spirit of song came mightily [1 Sam 10:10]
upon the Hebrews,
and who first opened its gates to them,
and went into its chambers
to gather its bdellium and its pearls;
and who split its streams and their pools,
and who measured their waters in the hollow of his hand,
and meted out their heavens with the span [Isa 40:12]."
And they spoke at length, each man after his own way,
one saying this and another saying that [1 Kgs 22:20].
And there was found an old man and wise,
and he sat among them,
listening to their words,
and it could be seen from the look of his eyes that he laughed and mocked at them.
And when they had given and taken in their words,
and roamed about to seek and did not find,
and toiled to come out of the sea of folly, and sank,
and the men rowed hard to bring it back to the dry land, and could not [Jonah 1:13],
he said to them: "Woe to you who have come into a river that has risen and swelled,
and sweeps on and passes over [Isa 8:8]!
You are entangled in the land, with no crossing;
the wilderness has shut you in [Exod 14:3].
The question you have asked is farther than the sky,
and deeper than the sea,

and there is no man but me who knows its secret
and reaches to its foundation."

Says the narrator: When the people heard his speech,
they all gathered around him,
for him to kindle his spark for them
and drop his showers upon them.
He said to them: "Know that the wondrous poetry,
which is filled with pearls,
was at first the inheritance of the Arabs.
They repaired every breach in it [2 Kgs 12:6],
and weighed it in the balances of justice [Job 31:6],
and set it up on its base,
and raised its tabernacle,
and set its table in order,
and fed others its portion.
They built its upper chambers,
and walked about its palaces [Ps 48:14];
they laid its beams and set up its doors [Neh 3:3].
Theirs is the highest rank over the poets of the world,
and when the men of poetry are called upward [Hos 11:7], they answer and go up first.
And though there are poets in every nation too,
who busy themselves with the craft of song,
all their songs, beside the songs of the Ishmaelites, are neither a help nor a profit [Isa 30:5],
and all their words are vanity, with nothing in them that profits [Jer 16:19].
For poetry pleasant in its utterance,
sweet in its reading,
like the sun at its height,
and sure as the dawn in its going forth [Hos 6:3],
belongs to the Arabs alone,
and all the nations are as nothing beside them [Isa 40:17].
Only, the children of our people, after their exile from their land,
many of them dwelt with the Arabs in their countries,
and grew used to speaking their tongue
and thinking their thoughts;
and when they mingled with them,
they learned the craft of poetry from them,
as it is said: They mingled among the nations and learned their works [Ps 106:35].
For while our fathers still dwelt in the Holy City,
no metered poem in the holy tongue was known to them,
only the books of Job, Proverbs, and Psalms,
which are short and light verses;
they look like the lines of a poem, but they have no rhyme and are not metered.
And because a remnant of the tribe of Judah escaped to Spain,
as it is said: And the exiles of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad [Obad 1:20];
and the land of Spain lies over against the middle of the sky, straight and level,
under the equal and straight line ⚠1;
for the people of Babylon are on the equal line in the east,
and the people of Spain at the end of the equal line in the west;
therefore the sciences spread at these two ends, each after its kind,
for they are on the two sides of the sphere, and its pillars,
on which the house stands [Judg 16:26].
In these two places the world took its glory and its strength:
it shall have two shoulder-pieces joined at its two ends [Exod 28:7].
For of old there were in Babylon
the sages of the world,
and likewise in Spain there were
masters of the sciences in every science, and in the secret of poetry, joined and parted ⚠2.
Therefore their tongue was kept in its purity;
its taste remained in it, and its scent was not changed [Jer 48:11].
Poetry had a camp among the men of Spain;
before they call it, it answers [Isa 65:24];
from their heart it journeys and at their mouth it encamps [Num 9:18]."
And he took up his parable and said:

When the sages hid from singing a song, / the sages of Spain mustered to sing it.
And when the people of the East found no vision [Lam 2:9] in song, / the sons of the West prophesied.

"It came to pass in the year four thousand seven hundred of the Creation ⚠3,
in the seventh century, that the spirit of counsel and might [Isa 11:2] came mightily upon the people of Spain,
and a pure language [Zeph 3:9].
Then the Hebrews began
to train their tongue in the ways of song,
and the coals of eloquence began to set their hearts alight,
and the signal-smoke began to go up out of the city [Judg 20:40].
But their eloquence at that time was poor,
and their tongue limped on the path of poetry,
until the eighth century came;
and then they roused themselves to play upon the Sheminith [Ps 6:1] ⚠4,
and prepared the house of poetry according to its rule and its pattern [Exod 25:9],
and measured its plan [Ezek 43:10].
In those days there shone in Spain the sun of praise
in the firmament of greatness,
the great Nasi,
Rabbi Isaac the Sephardi ben Ḥasdai ⚠5,

may he rest in the shadow of the Almighty [Ps 91:1];
for he poured out to all who asked of him a blessing past all measure [Mal 3:10].
In his days the waves of the sciences roared,
and their waters cast up crystal and coral [Job 28:18],
and their streams stood up like walls [Exod 15:8].
Every soul that thirsted for the waters of wisdom quenched its thirst in him;
and God opened her eyes,
and she saw a well of water, sweeter than honeycomb to the lips,
and she went and filled the skin with water [Gen 21:19].
For that Nasi revived with the dews of his kindness the dead of folly,
and drew with the cords [Hos 11:4] of his bounty the hearts of the children of the exile,
and he made a proclamation [Exod 36:6]: 'Whoever is for the Lord, to me [Exod 32:26]!
All his wants are upon me [Judg 19:20].'
And there gathered to him every Gaon and every master,

from the lands of Edom and Arabia,
from east and from west;
and he set the table of his kindness before them,
and the cloud of his majesty stood over them,
and the pillar of fire of his glory lit their eyes [Num 14:14].
The sages of the generation gathered to him, shining with lights of brightness,
to set in array the order of wisdom before those who seek God;
and he filled them with the spirit of God,
in wisdom, in understanding, and in knowledge poured out,
and in all manner of workmanship,
to devise devices [Exod 31:3–4],
and to hew from the hearts
blazing flames [Ps 29:7].
From that time the sciences broke forth in Spain,
and from these the whole earth was overspread [Gen 9:19].

From then on the murmurers learned instruction [Isa 29:24],
and the singers went before, the players after [Ps 68:26].
For his kindness made the tongue of the dumb sing [Isa 35:6],
and opened the hearts that were stopped up,
until they composed pleasant poems in his honor,
that shine like the stars on high.
Then the eyes were opened in the craft of poetry,
and the heavens were opened,
and visions of God were seen [Ezek 1:1].
In his days wisdom spread in Israel,
for he was to learning a kinsman and a redeemer [Ruth 2:1, 2:20] ⚠6.
And after that time
the lights that had shone grew dim,

and the pillars that had stood high fell,
and the Nasi died, and all his brothers, and all that generation [Exod 1:6].
Then came the ninth century, and in it was born Rabbi Isaac ibn Khalfun,
who filled with his poems the western side and the north,
and he was the first to pass over into the ranks of poetry.
God gave him, in some of his poems, the answer of the tongue [Prov 16:1].
He began to be a mighty one [Gen 10:8] in poems,
and he opened its closed doors
to the children of the Hebrews;
then they went down to the gates [Judg 5:11].
Yet only a few of his poems are goodly as the fruit of a goodly tree [Lev 23:40],
and most of them are thorns and thistles [Gen 3:18].
There were many poets in his generation,
who growled like bears [Isa 59:11],
but his poetry rose above them all,
and he was anointed king over their assembly.
In his days there lived the great Nagid, Rabbi Samuel ha-Levi, of blessed memory, who bared a high arm [Job 38:15] in the craft of poetry,
and brought hidden things out to light [Job 28:11].
At the end of his days was born Rabbi Solomon the Small ⚠7, who waved the spices of poetry, and there came no more such spice [1 Kgs 10:10];
and after him there arose none like him [2 Kgs 23:25].
For all the poets who came after him
ran to learn from his poems,
and did not reach the dust of his feet [Nah 1:3],
for the force of his verses
and the strength of his words.
Had he lived long,
he would have composed mighty wonders in the secrets of poetry;
but his crown was plucked while still in its bloom [Job 8:12],
and he was snatched away while the freshness of youth was in him [Deut 34:7].
At twenty-nine his lamp went out,
and he did not reach thirty.
In the course of the ninth century those poets perished; only their delights remained.
They were gathered to their people [Gen 25:8], and their praises lived on.
And all that generation too were gathered to their fathers [Judg 2:10].
After them arose the generation of the lovely poets,
and their time was called the time of the blossoms [Song 2:12],
for the generation was like thorns and they like lilies [Song 2:2].
That is the generation of the wondrous poets,
who are called a band of prophets [1 Sam 10:5],
among whom was Rabbi Judah ha-Levi,
the turtledove of the Torah ⚠8 and the ornament of the Testimony,
and the other poets of his generation, peace be upon them; and though none of them reached in his poems the rank of Rabbi Solomon,
nor attained the force of his speech
and the sweetness of his taste,
yet they had wondrous words
and awesome rhymes,
and through them were seen the awesome works of the sweet poems,
as if they were taken from the mouth of cherubim,
or stolen from the tongue of seers.
The spirit rested upon them, and they were of those that were written [Num 11:26],
and they prophesied, and did not cease [Num 11:25];
and to hear the wonders of their song the princes of the peoples gathered [Ps 47:10]."
And he took up his parable and said:

Nobles who put on garments of delight, / they shone like the sun in the heavens of the Testimony.
At their song men of heart were dismayed, / and Time wrapped itself in garments of trembling [Ezek 26:16].
They set up boards of understanding for their song, / and in their hearts sockets for it, and tenons [Exod 26:17–19].
They were unique among the people of God, / and so too their eloquence was unique.
Prophecy spoke with them mouth to mouth, / in the vision of song, plainly and not in riddles [Num 12:8].

"After that generation the springs of song were stopped up
in the Hebrew tongue,
and among the fat ones of the eloquence of poetry leanness was sent [Isa 10:16];
her prophets, too, found no vision [Lam 2:9].
All who came after that generation toiled to search out the spring of song,
and hewed out broken cisterns [Jer 2:13],
and their waters were bitter [Exod 15:23].
And since Solomon, who sat on his throne, was gathered in,
and Abraham his Nasi breathed his last and died [Gen 25:8],
and Judah the captain of his host,
and Moses his prophet ⚠9,
the vision of poetry and its source were stopped up,
and that glory and its awe departed,
and the angel of the Lord appeared no more [Judg 13:21].
For none arose after them
who could compose like their poems.
And here we are, all of us here this day [Deut 5:3], gleaning the forgotten sheaves of their ears [Deut 24:19],
and cutting off the stragglers behind them [Deut 25:18].
Day and night we run in their tracks,
and toil, and do not touch them ⚠10.
For each one of us meditates a vain thing [Ps 2:1],
and draws out the sword of folly [Lev 26:33].
The first ones ate the fine flour,
and left us the bran.
And indeed I have seen the communities of the East,
where the glory of the Lord has shone [Isa 60:1],
and the communities of France,

and Ashkenaz and Riphath [Gen 10:3],
and the communities of Persia,
and Meshech and Tiras [Gen 10:2],
and the communities of Greece,
who dwell between Dishon and Alvan [Gen 36:21–23] ⚠11:
there is no wisdom that they have not inherited,
and no good quality that they have not conquered;
only the land of poetry alone they have seen and not entered.
They thought they had learned the craft of rhymes,
and supposed they had understood it;
but poetry is far from them and will not come near them,
as far as the east is from the west [Ps 103:12].
Their soul goes round about its land and its dwelling,
but it shall not pass through it;
for you shall see the land from afar, but you shall not go there [Deut 32:52].

If you ask them, they will say that there are no poets like them,
and no poems like their poems.
But no man sees his own blemish,
and no man finds himself guilty.
For poetry has seven conditions, and by them it is sweet and pleasant,
and its ways are ways of pleasantness [Prov 3:17];
and whoever does not keep these conditions, his poem shall be trampled,
and shall be like the layer of dew [Exod 16:13]: when the sun grows hot, it melts [Exod 16:21].

"The first condition: the poet must purge the words of his poems of all dross,
and remove every strange word from his words,
lest he be like the poets of Greece, who fill their poems with a strange word next to a precious word,
and join blossoms
with thorns,
and pearls with stones,
and mother-of-pearl
with thistles.
Therefore all their poems are altered,
the language slack and the tongues mixed.

"The second condition: the poet must keep the meter of the poem and its vowels,
and not add, and not ⚠12 take away [Deut 13:1] from the meter of his sayings,
lest he be like the poets of our generation, whose meters are broken,
at times short,
at times too long;
they pay no heed to the meter,
and do not know whether it is heavy or light.

"The third condition: it is fitting for the poet that the themes of his poem be strong,
cast in the thickness of eloquence,
lest he be like the poets of the land of Shinar, whose themes are withered and thin [Gen 41:23],
and empty of good eloquence.
There is no man among them who draws deep waters [Prov 20:5] out of his mind,
for the rope is cut, the bucket torn, the water spilt, and the vessels empty.

"The fourth condition: the poet's poems must be easy to understand,
and all of them plain to him that understands [Prov 8:9],
lest he be like the poets of France, all of whose poems are full of confusion
and need a commentary.

"The fifth condition: the poet must keep his poem from errors of grammar,
lest he be like the poets of Damascus,
and above all Isaac ben Baruch the physician, all of whose poems are like a broken potsherd,
and like straw without grain [Jer 23:28].
All his words are strange,
his waters bitter,
his themes colder than snow,
his heroes weaklings,
his men women,
and his holy ones harlots ⚠13.
His words are full of mistakes,
in words and in letters.
And he trusts in the fools who praise his poems,
and heal his pangs lightly [Jer 6:14];
and they do not know whether he sings for joy
or laments,
and whether he is a poet
or a braying ass.

"The sixth condition: when the poet has finished his poem, he must not hurry to show it
until he has refined it.
Let him not leave its scum upon it [Ezek 24:6],
nor its filthiness in its skirts [Lam 1:9];
but let him remove from it all its shams ⚠14,
and take away all its alloy [Isa 1:25],
lest he be like the foolish poets, who show the poem before the assembly and the congregation [Prov 5:14] before they have finished it,
and their device gives birth before it is in labor [Isa 66:7].

"The seventh condition: the poet must choose part of his poem
and cast away part;
let him choose for himself, out of fifty,
thirty,
and out of seventy,
forty.
Then his poem will be clean,
like a very fair bride [Jer 46:20].
By these conditions wisdom and knowledge are glorified:
she has hewn out her seven pillars [Prov 9:1].
By these measures the craft of poetry was subdued to the people of Spain,
and she became their wife [Gen 24:67],
and without them she is divorced;
with them she was betrothed and holy,
and with others profaned and a harlot [Lev 21:7].
And know that the sons of men are divided
into three parts:
a band of fools,
a band of the wise,
and a band of discerning poets.
Therefore every poet must put into his poem plain and easy themes,
so that they please the fools;
and strong themes and force of words,
so that they please the wise;

and clean workmanship
and very fair eloquence,
so that it pleases the poets,
who know the choicest of poems.
Therefore you will find the poems of the poets divided
into these parts.
For there is a poem that has the first kind of theme, and it pleases the fools
and not the wise;
and a poem that has the second kind of theme pleases the wise
and not the fools;
and a poem that has the third kind of theme pleases the poets
and not the others.
But the poem that gathers all three of these kinds has nothing above it,
like the poems of Rabbi Judah ha-Levi, of blessed memory, in which are all three kinds; for you will find his poems plain and smooth,
tender and strong,
sweet and pleasing.
The poetry of Rabbi Solomon the Small pleases the wise
and not the fools;
and the poetry of Rabbi Moses ibn Ezra pleases the poets
more than the others,
for its eloquence
and the pleasant taste of its workmanship.
And I have seen, in the regions of the West, lands that border Spain on two sides.

On the one side are the regions of Provence, and they have deep poems,
and their themes are good and strong,
but they are not sweet and pleasing like the poems of Spain.
On the other side are the lands of the Ishmaelites,
which are under the kingdom of the rebel king ⚠15, may his name be blotted out,
and may his fall soon spring forth [Isa 58:8].
There, before the time of the persecution, were the sages of the world,
all of them in the measures of wisdom;
only the craft of poetry was weak and thin,
neither strong nor sweet.
I did not see among them a strong poem,

cast in the thickness of the intellect,
except the poems of the supreme sage,
Rabbi Joseph ben Judah ben Simeon ⚠16;
for the workmanship of his poetry is clean and strong,
and engraved upon the heart.
His poetry alone is a small measure, but clean [b. Yevamot 49b],
and the poems of others are full of vomit [Isa 28:8].
His is the very fair maqama,
built for an armory [Song 4:4],
which begins: 'Thus says Tobiah ben Zedekiah.'
In it he showed a forceful tongue,
by it the vision of poetry broke forth [1 Sam 3:1],
and before it no man moves his tongue [Exod 11:7];
that you may know how the Lord makes a distinction between him
and the poets of his time [Exod 11:7].
Apart from his poems I have not seen in the regions of the West
a poem that is pleasant and sweet;
but the poetry of others is cut off in the lifetime of its owners,
for there is not a house where there is not one dead [Exod 12:30].
And I saw among the people of France sages
shining like the stars on high,
their heart as the great sea in every science;
they left nothing, small or great, unlearned.
But the poetry they compose,
I hearkened and heard, but they speak not aright [Jer 8:6];
for their themes are despised,
and not fit to be heard.
Their poems are hard as iron,
laden with iniquities like the goat for Azazel [Lev 16:22];
their rhymes are full of confusion,
and they go deep in their poems to make novelties,
and no man understands them except with a commentary;
and there are some of them who make a commentary on their poems, and the commentary needs a commentary.
Therefore their poems are strange,
and their meters broken.
So too the communities of Greece: all of them are men of intellect and wisdom,
of knowledge and discretion,
uprightness and integrity,
and every pleasant quality.
But their poems are loathsome,
heavy as a burdensome stone [Zech 12:3],
like the cluster-tree that is good for nothing [Jer 13:10],
or a barren tree without blossom,
with neither taste nor scent [Lev. Rab. 30:12].
At times one of them escapes, whose mind God has stirred up,
such as the poems of Michael ben Caleb of the city of Thebes;
for his poems have some pleasantness upon them,
because he went to Spain in his youth and learned from them.
And so Rabbi Moses ben Avtalyon:
his poetry is supreme among the men of his generation.
And when I passed into the lands of the East, I saw communities there
rising to the height of praise,
the good qualities made perfect in them;
but all their poetry is like a broken potsherd,
or a hot wind of the bare heights in the wilderness,
not to fan and not to cleanse [Jer 4:11].
Many of them are like lost sheep [Jer 50:6],
empty of understanding and of testimony.
They conceive children in their poems
that are loathsome as a polluted garment [Isa 64:5],
and the flocks bring forth streaked, speckled, and spotted [Gen 30:39, 31:10].
In the city of No-amon ⚠17 I saw Elazar the Discerning,
and his poetry is helped by emptiness,
and girded with a girdle of vanity.
At times there are found in his poetry some themes
that are good and right.
And how my compassion yearned [Gen 43:30] for the poems whose glory the Egyptians spoil,
and rule over them with rigor and afflict them [Exod 1:11–13];
I have also seen the oppression with which the Egyptians oppress them [Exod 3:9].
Except for the poems of Rabbi Abraham who dwells in Damira, for some of his poems took refuge in the shade [Judg 9:15] of the intellect,
and were lifted up like a banner on the mountain [Isa 30:17],
and the rest fled to the mountain [Gen 14:10].
In Damascus too I saw honored men,
with good qualities and kindnesses,
and their deeds are lovely;
only, the poems they compose are full of scum,
an unclean spirit, polluted and foul.

If they threw them to the swine to eat,
the swine would hold them forbidden.
And the choicest of their poets is Isaac ben Baruch the physician, and all his poems are reckless and empty,
for the emptiers have emptied them [Nah 2:3].
Yet they are sweet to fools as a bundle of myrrh [Song 1:13],
and their words please an ass.
And there are poets among them who steal the poetry of Rabbi Judah and Rabbi Solomon,
and change its sense,
and spoil their meters,
and add strange words to them,
and strip them of their embroidered garments,
and put the filthy garments [Zech 3:4] on them,
and join to the chief spices [Exod 30:23]
wormwood and gall [Deut 29:17] ⚠18,
and to bdellium and mother-of-pearl,
thorn and thistle [Gen 3:18];
and they sing them at their drinking feasts,
and tell the fools that these are their own poems.
And among the poets of Damascus there is an Egyptian who composes poems forbidden to the ear,
and whoever hears them is bound to rend his garments.
They are like the vile figs that cannot be eaten, they are so bad [Jer 29:17];
I never saw such in all the land of Egypt for badness [Gen 41:19].
And he thinks, in his pride and his arrogance,
that Solomon the Small is too small to contain his thought,
and Abram very heavy [Gen 13:2] in his poems beside the purity of his own tongue,

and Moses was keeping his flock [Exod 3:1],
and Judah shall acknowledge [Gen 49:8] the wisdom of his thoughts ⚠19,
and all the giant poets are as grasshoppers in his eyes [Num 13:33].
He it is who says in his poem,
laying bare his shame:
"And my poem is like a Sephardi's poem, and everything in its season."
O that the Sephardi's soul would come back to him [1 Kgs 17:21],
and Solomon sit on the throne of his kingdom [Deut 17:18],
until he saw the filth of his poem
and its unclean spirit!
For he would have crushed his brain,
shattered his head [Jer 23:29],
and cut off his tail;
dragged him by his foot,
turned him on his face,
and thrown him into the privy.
I also saw among the poets of Damascus a Hebrew man
smiting an Egyptian man [Exod 2:11]:
he is the son of Baruch the physician, Isaac,
with whom poetry went down to a far land.
It fell on its face and laughed [Gen 17:17],
and said: 'God has made laughter for me; whoever hears will laugh at me [Gen 21:6] ⚠20;
for loathsome lads laugh at me,
and wanton boys make sport of me.'
This Isaac just named is the one who reviles the poems of the Egyptian,
and he answered and said:

Turn to me, glory of rulers and princes, / and do not turn to the poems of others.
Hew a grave for their poems and bury them, / and if they are many, hew them graves.

"And I wonder how one who is buried can hew a grave for others;
a prisoner cannot free himself from the prison house [b. Berakhot 5b].
One should say to him: 'You have multiplied words against your brother [Ezek 35:13],
and seen his blemish, while your own blemishes are hidden from you.
Of you the upright sages of Israel said:
Your own you do not see, but you see those of others.
And while you hew graves for the Egyptian's poem,
is not your poem one that Egyptians bury [Num 33:4]?
For all of it is like dung on the dunghill,
neither a help nor a profit, but a shame and also a reproach [Isa 30:5].'
And I saw the people of Zobah ⚠21,
may God remember them for good [Neh 5:19],
for they have gathered every good quality;
but the poetry they compose fell into their hands like a bird in a net,
and sank into slime pits [Gen 14:10];
and when they sing it,
it laments: 'Behold, for peace I had great bitterness [Isa 38:17].'
There was at first a poet there named Rabbi Joseph bar Ṣemaḥ,
and he had good qualities, but his poetry was a stalk ⚠22
that yields no meal [Hos 8:7].
And among their poets was Rabbi Shelah;
he took the craft of poetry for a bride,
and all his days she was with him a virgin, never known.
And she cries out with a wounded soul,
for she saw that Shelah was grown, and she was not given to him as a wife [Gen 38:14].
After him arose Yaḥya ibn al-Qaṣir, a sweet cantor,
who draws near to the tabernacle of the Lord [Num 17:28].
He is a man of beloved qualities
and well-ordered deeds,
but his poetry, like the other poets of his generation, did not turn from their way,
nor stray from their path.
There are other poets like him,
and all their poems are wind and emptiness [Isa 41:29],
some of them new and some old,
some cantors and some judges,
and their poems are nothing and emptiness [Isa 40:17].
Some of them glory in the poems of Ibn ʿAbbas ⚠23, and say that after him none arose who could compose a poem.
But if God would take the heart of stone out of them [Ezek 36:26],
and the eyes of their thoughts were opened
to know the supreme poetry,
which dismays every thought,
they would know that they did not know the truth,
and understand that they could not tell the living from the dead.
And I saw in the city of Calneh, which is al-Raqqa ⚠24, a man who goes up to the highest rank of poetry,
and he is of those of the first watch ⚠25.
He opens wide his mouth in the craft of poetry,
and he is a braying ass.
He knows nothing of wisdom and understands nothing
but fodder and straw;
he has learned nothing of understanding and knowledge,
only to bear the saddle and the saddle-cloth.
His poetry is good in the eyes of his congregation,
and lovely to the mind of his community;
for the beast and the vessels
are as the feet of their owners [m. Beitzah 5:3] ⚠26.

His name is Berakhot ben Yeshuʿa,
and it is sheer terror to understand the report [Isa 28:19].
And of him I said:

Curses ⚠27! He composes a poem like filth, / and the filth in his mouth he reckons as honeycomb.
His thought lays bare his disgrace, / and his nakedness is uncovered in his poem.
His father was a slave to villainy, / and his mother was reviled with reproaches.
Both of them were gathered to their eternity, / but their reproach, a sign for all, is not gathered in.
And the son is a witness to the uncleanness of his fathers, / like a foul smell over the privy.

"And among the prophets of Arbel I saw a horrible thing [Jer 23:14]:
one of their poets took the closings of the ʿAnaq of Rabbi Moses ibn Ezra ⚠28,
and on every closing of his rhymes he composed a whole poem,
like dung on the dunghill, trodden down.
He fashioned it with a graving tool and made it a molten calf [Exod 32:4].
His name is called Ben ha-Sekhvi ⚠29;
and whence shall understanding come [Job 28:20] to the birds of the field,
or who will give understanding to the cock [Job 38:36]?
And of the early poets of the East, Rav Shemariah
and the head of the academy Zechariah
were both mighty and honored in the Talmud,
fearing Heaven and pious.
Had they not made mention of poetry,
they would have done wisely and hidden their evil report [Gen 37:2].
But they took the garment of poetry between them,
and laid it upon the shoulders of both of them [Gen 9:23],

and went backward, and did not see the nakedness of their poems [Gen 9:23], and stripped off the robe of discipline they had worn,
and they were both naked and were not ashamed [Gen 2:25].
And I saw the poets of Babylon,
where the Geonim of the world were:
as high as they rose in the Testimonies,
so low they fell in their heavy poems.
Their poems lament: 'Woe to us, for we are undone [Isa 6:5],
and all of us like sheep have gone astray [Isa 53:6];
by the rivers of Babylon, there we sat, and wept,
and upon the willows in the midst of it we hung up our lyres [Ps 137:1–2].'
The choicest of their poets, Rabbi Isaac bar Israel, head of the academy: some of his poems are strong and good,
but most are counted as a broken potsherd,
neither sweet nor pleasing.
He also composed a book of maqamat,
and in it are poems and letters,
weak and cold,
as if cut from the snow of Senir.
All its themes are poor,
and it bred worms [Exod 16:20];
but had he only kept silent [Job 13:5],
there would have been no worm in it, and it would not have stunk [Exod 16:24].
After him arose Rabbi Isaac ibn al-Awani;
he is very rich, but his poetry is poor and needy.
He bought the academy for a thousand gold pieces, and another will soften her ⚠30:
you shall betroth a wife, and another man shall lie with her [Deut 28:30].
He composes poems shattered into the pit of nothing,
and the vessel is spoiled [Jer 18:4].
And if one asks: Which is the poem the wise despise?
The Lord says: Behold, it is hidden among the baggage [1 Sam 10:22].
And all the poets of Aden ⚠31 are dreamers, stunned,
asleep in the bosom of folly.
Among them are lads and youths
who broke down in their poems the words of the sages,
little foxes that spoil the vineyards [Song 2:15],
and the garment of their poetry is rolled in blood [Isa 9:4].
Rabbi Moses bar Sheshet, who went there from our land, led the people of Aden in the way of poetry,
that he might heal them of pain and every pang;
but no healing came up for them [Jer 8:22]:
we would have healed Babylon, but she was not healed [Jer 51:9]."
And he took up his parable and said:

True, all the people of the East are high, / and the sages of the time are below them, on the slope.
And all the sages of France and Greece, / their heart runs and trembles to the Testimonies.
Every wisdom and understanding they took for themselves, / and left poetry to the people of Spain.

Says the narrator: When I heard the wonders of his sayings
and the pleasantness of his words,
I asked him his name and the land of his birth,
and he answered and said:

I am Ḥever, a companion to all who seek me, / and Elon-bezaanannim [Judg 4:11] is the city of my dwelling.
In the battle of understanding my praises are my weapons, / and I have made my tongue my trident ⚠32.

When I heard his speech,
I knew his name,
and came to ask after his welfare;
but he was in haste to go [1 Sam 23:26],
and parted from me, and my soul is drawn by the cords of his love [Hos 11:4].
