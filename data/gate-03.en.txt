Thus says Heman the Ezrahite: In the days of my youth I set my heart,
in the time of my young manhood,
on chasing pleasures with companions
upon the mountains of Bether [Song 2:17],
as one hunts the partridge in the mountains [1 Sam 26:20].
I bound my eyelids to the eyelids of the dawn [Job 3:9],
and my wings to the wings of eagles,
to take my delight in the villages [Song 7:12],
in a place of nard with henna [Song 4:13],
and a pasture of young deer.
For the times were still my slaves,
and their children went out in troops to serve me.
Before I called them they would answer [Isa 65:24],
and after my words they spoke no more [Job 29:22].
The children of a day were led by the bridle of my desire;
wherever the spirit was to go, they went [Ezek 1:12].
But when the troop sounded the trumpet blast [Lev 25:9]
to pull out the peg that was fastened [Isa 22:25],
I girded my loins with the belt of travel,
took off the garment of rest,
and put on the clothing of toil.
I rebuked love and chose to wander,
and set my aim to roam to the ends of the world,
until I came to the border of Shinar and Elam.
One day I was in one of the cities of Babylon,
going about like a ship without a pilot.
One of the sons of the nobles invited me to his feast,
one of the honored lords of the land,
and gave me a place to walk among those who stand by [Zech 3:7].
He brought me to a palace,
nursed in the bosom of beauty [Lam 4:5],
cut and hewn from the quarry of delight;
from its rocks flow honey and milk,
and within it are houses full of all good things [Deut 6:11], and wealth increases [Ps 62:11].
Around the court were castles,
and within the castles turrets,
with circlets of gold [Song 1:11] carved on the walls.
In them souls rejoice,
and ears tingle [1 Sam 3:11]
at the stir of the sound of the harp,
and the flow of the waters of the channel. And the houses were covered with settings of gold [Ps 45:14],
and every house adorned with all kinds of headbands [Isa 3:18],
and with embroidered sails [Ezek 27:7] spread over it,
and it had made itself a coat of many colors [Gen 37:3].
Within the houses were tables,
set with every good thing,
fat and fresh [Ps 92:15],
and around them carpets,
that renew the heart
and drive away grief.
Before us were flagons and jars,
holding delights for every eye,
and the wine like torches within the jars [Judg 7:16].
And the goblets
were firmaments for the stars of the wine,
and the basins
as if cast out of light,
and banded with the gold of desire ⚠1.
And I answered and said:

Marvel how coal and hail are joined together, / and flames of fire burst out of the channels,
in threads of chains of beaten gold, / cast in the clay of the heart's plain [1 Kgs 7:46] ⚠2.
They are juices in cups covered with pearl [Esth 1:6], / and they scatter lights up to the skies.
They water you with a dew of lights, and yet / into your mouth they pour streams of flame.
It is as if the goblets were firmaments, / and the wine within them like lightnings.

There were set before us all kinds of dainties,
and a feast of fat things [Isa 25:6],
and rich savory dishes.
Around us were lovely servants,
standing before us like lamps,
to bring every pleasant and beloved food;
each of them hurrying with a vessel of shining brass [Ezra 8:27],
the cupbearer with a cup of gold in his hand.
And in came also the hilt after the blade [Judg 3:22] ⚠3,
with roasted kids in his hands,
and gathering lambs in his arm [Isa 40:11],
tender and fair,
whom the heat of the fire had tormented ⚠4
until it brought them back
bright and clean,
moist and tender,
crushing the pride of hunger,
and striking its pride with the scourge of the tongue [Job 5:21].
Among the guests [1 Sam 9:22] was an old man,
wandering like a bird from its nest [Prov 27:8].
He reclined at the laid table,
coiled like a serpent,
with a long arm
to bring near every far-off dish,
and to grind it and pound it fine.
He had a palate
that licked up all that was round about us [Num 22:4],
and a tongue
that swept the food away like the brook Kishon [Judg 5:21],
and he had a mouth
that neither cupbearer nor baker could fill.
Always he set his eye on the cup [Prov 23:31],
and his tooth in the bread,
and the goblet was found going round to his right hand [Hab 2:16].
And the meat,
even if it were bound in fetters of iron [Ps 149:8],
he would draw to him with his tongue,
and let it go free for his tooth [Exod 21:27].
He took the fat of every bread,
and the best of every dish,
and into his mouth he pushed and thrust
every good piece, the thigh and the shoulder [Ezek 24:4].
The fat meat he drew with both hands,
and the lips gathered it up,
and ground it in the mill [Num 11:8], while his arms held up the bowl
and drew to him all that was in it,
and out of his mouth went torches [Job 41:11].
And the pieces,
the choice ones,
if he took them in his palm,
he sent upon them the fierceness of his anger [Ps 78:49].
And every fat slice
that he saw from afar,
he drew with cords of love [Hos 11:4],
and while it was still in his hand, he swallowed it [Isa 28:4].
The lambs
feared the sword of his teeth,
the sheep
fled from dread of him,
and the fish
were anxious for terror of him.
And the meat, while it was yet between his teeth [Num 11:33], cried out before all living things:
"The Lord has given me into hands from which I cannot rise [Lam 1:14]."
He was like a creditor over the dish,
and a lender over the cauldron [1 Sam 2:14];
and when they came to the table, the creditor came to take [2 Kgs 4:1],
until he wiped the dish [2 Kgs 21:13],
and drained the cruse,
above and below,
and still the sack was not filled.
He honored the wine like his father,
kissed the cup like his brother,
and put his mouth upon its mouth [2 Kgs 4:34].
He did not rest
until he had gleaned all there was.
And the food,
he ate it;
and the broth,
he tossed it into his mouth.
He left nothing at all before us.
The land was like the garden of Eden before him, and behind him a desolate wilderness [Joel 2:3].
At last, for the evil of his deeds,
our anger nearly rose against him,
and if modesty had not held us back, we would have thrown him off the table,
and driven him out of the house.
And we said to one another:
"Have you ever seen such a man,
whose lip
is his spear,
and his tongue
his pitchfork,
and his teeth
his weapons ⚠5?"
And he knew from the look in our eyes
the hidden thoughts we had,
and heard some of our talk;
but in the sweetness of eating he took the hurt and the sting,
and spoke to us neither good nor bad [2 Sam 13:22].
And we, in the strength of that food [1 Kgs 19:8], were talking about the craft of poems,
and inquiring into the poets
who had been in the Hebrew tongue,
and the man listened and kept silent;
but at the bowls he struck like an adder [Prov 23:32],
and looked at us with an eye of mockery and scorn,
and lorded it over us as the high over the needy,
or as a creditor
over a man poor and hard pressed [Isa 8:21].
And when he had struck the bowls with stones of stumbling and of ruin,
and they were all cleanness of teeth to him [Amos 4:6],
and with his eating had quieted the failing of the eyes and the sickness of the mind [Deut 28:65],
"and both of them died, Mahlon and Chilion [Ruth 1:5]" ⚠6,
then he lifted his eyes to us and looked down at us from the heavens of pride.

He turned to the one who sat at his left and said to him: "These lost and straying sheep [Jer 50:6],
tell me, where do they graze [Song 1:7]? And what are they talking about,
and what matter are they recounting?"

I said to him: "They are talking about the champions of poetry who were in Spain,
and the power of their eloquence, in joined verse and in single lines ⚠7.
And they all agreed and decided,
and said with one mouth,
that no poems are as strong as those of Solomon ⚠8,
and none deeper than the poems of the Nagid,
and none so sweet and smooth as the poems of Ibn Ḥasdai,
and none so cast in the furnace of the intellect as the poems of Ibn Taqana,
and none so divided into good and bad as the poems of Ibn Khalfun,
and none so far beyond understanding as the poems of Isaac ibn Ghiyyat,
and none so well tried as the poems of Rabbi Joseph ibn Shatanash ⚠9,
and none so ancient as the poems of Rabbi Moses ha-Kohen ⚠10,
and none so kissed by the mouth of the hearts as the poems of Ibn Baruch ⚠11,
and none so held in the embrace of grace as the poems of Rabbi Adonim ⚠12,
and none so engraved on the heart as the poems of Rabbi Judah ibn Ghiyyat,
and none so refined as the poems of Rabbi Moses ibn Ezra,
and none so close to the soul as the poems of Rabbi Moses ibn Sheshet ⚠13,
and none so desired as the poems of Ibn al-Tabban,
and none so burning as the poems of Rabbi David ibn Bakoda,
and none so wrested from the host on high as the poems of Ibn Reuben ⚠14,
and none so firmly cast as the poems of Rabbi Abraham al-Ḥarizi ⚠15,
and none that bring forth from kind to kind [Ps 144:13] like the poems of Rabbi Joseph ibn Sahl,
and none so bright in the skies [Job 37:21] as the poems of Rabbi Abraham ibn Ezra,
and none so banded with the gold of desire as the poems of Ibn al-Muʿallim,
and none such necklaces for every neck [Prov 1:9] as the poems of Ibn Barzel,
and none so faithful and righteous as the poems of Ibn Ṣaddiq,
and none so much sweeter than honey [Judg 14:18] as the poems of Rabbi Judah Halevi."

When he heard my words, he was silent at my words for about a moment.
After that he lifted his eyes to me
and said to me: "Did you not hate me, and because of my eating
despise my greatness?
With your bad manners you almost laid a hand on me,
and drew the sword of the wicked against me [Ps 37:14].
Were it not for the humility that subdues me,
and binds me with the bridle of good manners,
I would have left you drowning in the sea of folly,
and sinking in the mire of simpleness,
so that you might reap your work,
and eat the fruit of your sin [Prov 1:31].
But I will not do it,
and your sin I will put away from my heart;
God will repay its reward to the one who does it,
and the one who offers it for sin shall eat it [Lev 6:19]."

The teller said: When the people heard the sweetness of his words,
they trembled at the thunder of his sayings,
and understood from his lightnings his rains,
and knew from his dawns his lights,
and knew that after his lightnings come clouds [Jer 10:13],
full of the rains of the intellect.
And for what they had done they were ashamed before him and confounded,
and were struck dumb from answering,
and laid their hand on their mouth [Mic 7:16].

He answered and said to them: "Men of understanding, hear me [Job 34:10],
and open your mouth wide for the latter rain [Job 29:23] of my intellect,
for I will loosen for you every seal,
and open for you every closed thing.
As for the poets you have named, whose secret you have searched out,
I happened by chance [2 Sam 1:6] to be in that battle set in array, and I am he who came from the battle line [1 Sam 4:16].
My heart is the scroll of their matters,
and I am the book of remembrance [Mal 3:16] for their poems.
And now incline your ear to me,
hear, and your soul shall live [Isa 55:3]."
And all the people gave ear to him,
and listened to his words.

He answered and said: "Know that the lofty poetry,
that is set with pearls,
and cannot be valued with the gold of Ophir [Job 28:16],
survived from Spain,
and from there spread out over all the ends of the world.
For the poems of the people of Spain are strong and sweet,
as if hewn from the flame of fire;
their poets are like males,
and all the other poets of the world like females.
But in former days there were many poets among them,
who sprang up like willows,
and their lines were closed, and they were not singled out by name [Num 1:17],
such as Menaḥem ben Saruq, Dunash ben Labrat, Samuel, and Avun, and many others besides these, of whose poems there is no memory,
because of the weakness of their themes,
until the great Nagid came, Rabbi Samuel ha-Levi, of blessed memory, and his poems were princes and chiefs,
and his themes strong and new;
but most of his poems are deep and hard,
and need commentaries.
In his generation was Rabbi Isaac ibn Khalfun; some of his poems are strong,
and some are withered and thin, for the emptiers have emptied them [Nah 2:3].
Rabbi Joseph ibn Ḥasdai is the father of the orphan poem,
which begins: 'Has the gazelle such might, such vigor and strength?',
which is the only one of her mother [Song 6:9],
and there is none like her among her people.
The poems of Rabbi Moses ibn Taqana are set right by the hands of the intellect,
planted as pleasant plants [Isa 17:10].
And the poems of Rabbi Solomon the Small ⚠16:
before him every great one becomes small,
and none like him has arisen
since the days of Eber, the father of Joktan [Gen 10:25].
All the poets of his generation were emptiness and crookedness before him,
and the small one shall become a thousand [Isa 60:22].
He alone rose to the highest rank of poetry;
eloquence bore him upon the knees of understanding [Gen 50:23],
and bound a scarlet thread on his hand, saying: 'This one came out first [Gen 38:28].'
For all the poets who were before him, their poems beside his are wind and confusion [Isa 41:29],
and after him none like him arose [2 Kgs 23:25].
All who came after him learned from his taste,
and received the spirit of poetry from him,
and God anointed him king over the poems of his people.
'The Song of Songs, which is Solomon's [Song 1:1].'
It is enough for the strong poets
to understand his deep poems,
for his eloquence is too high for the knowledge of all of us [Ps 139:6],
and who shall go up for us to heaven and bring it to us [Deut 30:12]?
In the order for the fast days, too,
he showed mighty wonders,
and he composed an order for the Day of Atonement,
all its themes nard with henna [Song 4:13],
overlaid with sapphires [Song 5:14].
And the poems of Adonim
are sockets for the boards of the intellect [Exod 26:19].
And the poems of the master, Rabbi Isaac,
are engraved on the brow of glory.
In his book that he composed for the Day of Atonement he showed wonders,
which were a sign for him
that the spirit of prophecy had come mightily upon his poetry [1 Sam 10:6].
The poems of the Gaon Rabbi Joseph ibn Shatanash are pleasant and precious,
and he was the first in Spain to compose an order for the Day of Atonement.
And the poems of the master Baruch:
their fountain is blessed [Prov 5:18],
and their table laid for all who come.
The poems of Rabbi Judah ibn Ghiyyat, his brothers shall praise him [Gen 49:8],
and his praises bear witness to his praises.
Rabbi Moses ibn Ezra draws out [Exod 2:10] the pearls
from the depths of thought,
and the poem that he composed for the nights of supplication
makes the lips of sleepers speak [Song 7:10].
He too made an order for the Day of Atonement,
its words more precious than vessels of fine gold [Job 28:17].
And Rabbi Joseph ibn Sheshet
set up his poem as a mark [Lam 3:12], and became an archer [Gen 21:20].
Rabbi Levi and Rabbi Jacob ibn al-Tabban
thresh eloquence as straw is threshed [Isa 25:10].
And Rabbi David ibn Bakoda:
at his feet the fine phrases bow down,
and at the doors of his lips they keep watch [Prov 8:34].
The poems of Rabbi Isaac ben Reuben:
who is wise, that he may understand [Hos 14:10] their secret?
For he did wonders, making comely phrases,
and brought into the fullness of his rhymes the mention of all the commandments,
and set them in verses that cling together,
as if they were engraved by the holy spirit.
The poems of Rabbi Abraham ibn al-Ḥarizi are held fast in the hands of the intellect,
and 'your cheeks are comely with circlets, your neck with beads [Song 1:10].'
Rabbi Joseph ibn Sahl gathers up the rear of the camp of poetry [Num 10:25],
and fine phrases were born upon Joseph's knees [Gen 50:23].
His kinsman, Rabbi Solomon ibn Ṣaqbel,
he too learned the craft of poetry and received it.
He made the lovely tale in rhymed prose
that begins: 'Thus says Asher ben Judah.'
The poems of the sage Rabbi Abraham ibn Ezra are a help in troubles [Ps 46:2],
and generous rains in a time of drought [Ps 68:10];
all his liturgical poems are awesome,
and their themes wonderful;
the like of them beholders have not seen.
He too composed an order for the Day of Atonement, its themes cast in the furnace of the intellect,
and engraved on the hearts.
Isaac his son also drew from the fountain of poetry,
and on the son's poems lies some of the father's radiance.
But when he came to the lands of the East,
the glory of the Lord did not shine upon him [Isa 60:1],
and he took off from himself the precious robes of the faith,
and put off his garments, and put on other garments [Lev 6:4] ⚠17.
Rabbi Judah ibn Abbas too came from the lands of the West to the East,
and went on the road of poetry.
He has lovely and sweet poems,
and some of them are counted as nothing,
neither sweet nor good.
He also begot a violent son [Ezek 18:10],
who did outrageous villainy and gave great offense ⚠18.
The poems of Rabbi Solomon ibn al-Muʿallim:
his speech makes the tongue of the dumb sing [Isa 35:6],
and his light is not hidden from the blind.
The poems of Rabbi Joseph ibn Barzel are strong as iron,
and smoother than honey from the comb [Prov 5:3].
And among the nobles of Spain was the great prince Rabbi Isaac ibn Krispin, author of the Book of Ethics, a book all of whose poems are lovely,
bound together by the hand of the intellect,
so that they became one [Ezek 37:17].
Rabbi Joseph ibn Ṣaddiq, by the spirit of his poetry, tears the sea of knowledge into pieces,
rending mountains and breaking rocks [1 Kgs 19:11];
by the force of his words he moves the everlasting mountains [Hab 3:6],
and sweetens the waters of Marah [Exod 15:23];
and when all the daughters of song bow down, the horns of the righteous are lifted up [Ps 75:11].
And the poems of the Levite, Rabbi Judah,
are a garland of grace [Prov 1:9] for the head of the testimony,
and on its neck a row of carnelian and topaz [Exod 28:17].
He is the right-hand pillar [1 Kgs 7:21] of the house of poetry,
'he who sat in the seat, a Taḥkemonite [2 Sam 23:8]' ⚠19.
He is Adino the Eznite [2 Sam 23:8], and he lifted up his spear [2 Sam 23:18] against the giants,
and left the heroes of poetry slain.
All his poems dismay the heart of the wise,
and before him Asaph is almost swept away, and the hand of Jeduthun is gone [Deut 32:36] ⚠20,
and the song of the sons of Korah
has become a burden [Isa 1:14].
He went into the treasury of poetry and plundered its treasure house [Isa 39:2],
and took all its precious vessels,
and went out and shut the gate after him.
And all who went out in his footsteps
to learn the craft of his poems
did not reach the dust of his chariots.
All the poets take up his words [Deut 33:3],
and kiss the place of his feet.
For in the craft of the liturgical poem
his tongue is a polished [Isa 49:2] and sharpened arrow [Jer 9:7].
In his songs of praise, who can summon him to a contest [Jer 49:19]?
In his songs of prayer he draws every heart and conquers it.
In his songs of love his speech is like a layer of dew [Exod 16:13], and coals are kindled by it [2 Sam 22:9].
In his laments he makes the cloud of weeping pour, and bursts it open.
And if he composes a letter or a scroll,
you will find all fine eloquence gathered in it,
as if it were rolled down from the stars on high,
or drawn from the height of holiness.
In the tent of poetry the gates of the heights were opened to him,
for there God was revealed to him [Gen 35:7].
By the breath of his mouth [Ps 33:6] he stills the waves of wisdom [Ps 89:10],
and like a lion he shouts, yes, he roars [Isa 42:13],
and in all that he does he prospers [Ps 1:3]."
And he took up his parable and said:

To Levi all glory is joined and gathered, / and he is a garland of splendor on the head of its anointed.
And on the day his light shone in the circle of the West, / in the East every side was filled with his spices.
In the battle of the poem of his praises he set his weapons / to fight, and his words are his spears.
Many ran after him and grew weary / of finding his way, and did not know his paths.
He came alone into the chamber of poetry, / until he went out and shut its doors.
God had set bars upon its gates, / but he broke its bars with the hand of his intellect.
He made its plants sprout with the dew of his words, / and made its flowers bloom with the waters of his intellect.
Hearts are too narrow for his glory, / as tongues fall short of his praises.
And on the day the wise set in array the battles of poetry, / Judah is the one who prevailed among his brothers [1 Chr 5:2].

The teller said: When he had finished his words, he had made every tongue mute,
every wisdom hidden,
and every soul ashamed,
and they knew that all his judgments were righteous and true [Ps 19:10].
And the people asked him his name,
and where was the dwelling where he set his feet ⚠21.
He answered and said:

My dwelling is at the oak of Zaanannim [Judg 4:11] ⚠22, / and I have a soul whose seat is upon the head of the Bear [Job 9:9].
In its humility it bows down to the dust, / and the head of the Dragon ⚠23 bows down before its pride.
If eloquence has a head, I am its diadem, / and if it has a tongue, I am the answer of its tongue [Prov 16:1].

The teller said: When I heard the power of his poem and his thought,
and the purity of his tongue,
I knew that he was the one man of his generation, with none second to him:
our friend, Ḥever the Kenite.
We wished to take our delight in his love [Prov 7:18],
and to take pleasure in his company;
but he kept his enmity against us in his heart,
and spurned our company,
and went away in anger from our fellowship,
after he had confounded our thoughts,
and made our tongue cleave to our palate [Ezek 3:26].
And we were left ashamed for the evil we had done,
and we said: "Woe to us, for we are undone [Isa 6:5]!
Truly we are guilty concerning our brother [Gen 42:21]."
